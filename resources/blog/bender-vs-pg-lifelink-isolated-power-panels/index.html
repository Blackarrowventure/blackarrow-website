Bender vs. PG LifeLink: NFPA 99 Isolated Power Panels

Both meet the same UL and NFPA 99 baseline — what actually differs, and how to choose for a project

Black Arrow Venture company · Updated October 13, 2026

Hospitals shopping for an isolated power panel often start by asking which brand is more compliant. That's the wrong question — both Bender and PG LifeLink build to UL 1047 and UL 1022, the standards NFPA 99 and NEC 517 point to for isolated power systems and line isolation monitors. The real comparison is in the equipment itself: how the monitor behaves, what the panel includes, and what fits a given project.

Both Meet the Same Baseline — That's Not Where the Comparison Starts

UL 1047 covers isolated power systems equipment for health care facilities — the isolation transformer, the reference grounding bus, and the panel construction. UL 1022 covers the line isolation monitor that watches the circuit once it's installed. Both Bender and PG LifeLink build their hospital panels and monitors to these standards, and both are established suppliers to the US and international hospital market, with decades of installed base in operating rooms, cath labs, and critical care areas.

That shared baseline is exactly why this isn't a story about one brand being more compliant than the other. A panel from either manufacturer, correctly specified and commissioned, satisfies the same NFPA 99 requirement for a wet procedure location. Where they actually differ is in the monitor's feature set, the panel's construction and integration options, and the accessory ecosystem built around each — the things a design consultant or facility manager needs to know before specifying one over the other.

The Line Isolation Monitor: Where the Real Differences Show Up

Bender's standard hospital panel ships with the LIM2010 line isolation monitor: automatic self-calibration and self-check, a combined digital and analog bar-graph display, a selectable 2 mA or 5 mA alarm threshold, two programmable voltage-free alarm contacts, and an RS-485 connection for networking into a building management or nurse-call system. It pairs with Bender's MK2000 series remote indicators — stainless-steel-faced annunciators that duplicate the panel's safe/hazard alarm outside the room — and with the CP907 touch control panel for facilities that want isolated power status integrated into a broader OR control interface.

PG LifeLink's line isolation monitors are built on the same core idea — self-testing and self-calibrating operation that removes the need for routine manual testing, a dual analog/digital display, and a selectable 2 mA or 5 mA alarm threshold, with optional remote displays for areas outside the room. The two product lines solve the same monitoring problem with genuinely comparable core technology; the practical difference tends to show up in the networking and remote-annunciator ecosystem each manufacturer has built around its monitor, rather than in the monitoring itself.

Panel Construction and What Comes With It

Bender's isolated power panels pair a minimum 14-gauge galvanized steel backbox with a minimum 14-gauge type 304 stainless steel front trim, #4 brushed finish, and a hidden-hinge door with a flush-mounted key lock over the load center — panel construction aimed squarely at the clinical environment it's installed in. The RS-485 networking on the LIM2010 and the CP907 touch control option make Bender a straightforward fit where a facility wants isolated power status reporting tied into a wider building or OR management system.

PG LifeLink's panels are built to the same UL and CSA framework and are documented in use across a wider spread of room types — operating and surgical suites, cardiac catheterization labs, emergency rooms, and critical care and recovery areas — with the isolation transformer, line isolation monitor, breaker panel, and ground bus housed together as a single isolated distribution center. For a facility specifying isolated power across several different department types rather than just the OR, that breadth of documented application is a relevant data point.

What Actually Decides Which One Goes on a Project

In practice, three things settle the choice more often than a feature-by-feature scorecard. First, consistency with what's already installed: if a hospital's existing wings run Bender or PG LifeLink panels, matching the brand keeps remote indicators, spare parts, and staff training consistent across the facility — mixing brands mid-building adds complexity for no real benefit. Second, integration needs: a project that wants isolated power alarms networked into a BMS or nurse-call system leans toward whichever manufacturer's remote-annunciator and networking ecosystem the design consultant has already specified elsewhere. Third, the department mix: a project covering ORs alongside cath labs, ERs, and critical care may weight PG LifeLink's broader documented application range, while a project centered on OR suites with integrated control systems may weight Bender's CP907 and RS-485 options.

None of that makes one brand the default choice. Both pass the same UL and NFPA 99 requirements, both have a long track record in hospital installations, and the right one for a given project is the one that matches the existing installed base, the integration the design calls for, and the department mix the panel actually needs to serve.
