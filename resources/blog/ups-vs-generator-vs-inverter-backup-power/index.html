UPS vs Generator vs Inverter: Choosing Backup Power

Transfer time, runtime and the UPS types explained, and why most commercial and industrial sites need a UPS and a generator working together

Black Arrow Venture company · Updated November 10, 2026

When the power goes out, three different machines can keep a building running: a UPS, a standby generator and a battery inverter. They are often discussed as alternatives, but they answer different questions. A UPS answers "how do I avoid even a flicker?", a generator answers "how do I run for hours?", and an inverter answers "how do I keep a few loads on at low cost?". Choosing well starts with knowing which question your loads are asking.

Transfer Time and Runtime: The Two Numbers That Decide

Transfer time is how long the load is without power when the supply fails. An online UPS has none, because the load always runs from its inverter. A standby generator typically needs around ten seconds to start and take the load through an automatic transfer switch (ATS). Servers, medical equipment, PLCs and network gear will reset or lose data in far less time than that.

Runtime is how long backup lasts. A UPS usually supports minutes to an hour, limited by battery size and cost. A generator runs for as long as it has fuel. That is why the two are usually combined: the UPS covers the gap and short outages, and the generator covers long ones.

The Three UPS Types

The international UPS standard IEC 62040-3 groups UPS designs into three classes. Offline or standby UPS (VFD) passes mains straight through and switches to battery when it fails, with a short break; it suits single PCs. Line-interactive UPS (VI) adds automatic voltage regulation and suits small offices, network cabinets and point-of-sale.

Online double-conversion UPS (VFI) converts all incoming power to DC and back to clean AC, so the load is isolated from spikes, sags and frequency changes and sees no transfer at all. It is the standard choice for data rooms, hospitals, laboratories and industrial control, and for sites with unstable supply.

Where Generators and Inverters Fit

A diesel standby generator is the answer for long outages and large loads such as HVAC, pumps and lifts. It needs an ATS, fuel storage, regular exercise under load and maintenance. In Saudi summers, check the generator output at the real site temperature, since generators are derated at high ambient temperatures and altitude.

A battery inverter is closer to a large, simple UPS: it charges batteries from mains and runs selected loads during an outage, usually with a short transfer break. It suits homes, shops and small offices with modest loads. It is not a substitute for an online UPS on sensitive equipment, or for a generator on large plant.

Using a UPS and a Generator Together

In most commercial, healthcare and industrial buildings the right answer is both. The UPS carries critical loads through the generator start and through short dips, and the generator then supplies the UPS for the rest of the outage. This needs the two to be designed as one system: the generator must be sized for the UPS input, including battery recharge current, and the UPS must accept the generator frequency and voltage variation.

UPS manufacturers publish a recommended generator-to-UPS sizing ratio for each model; follow it rather than a rule of thumb. Test the full sequence, mains failure, generator start, transfer and return to mains, under real load during commissioning and then periodically.

Batteries in Saudi Heat

Battery temperature has a large effect on life. VRLA batteries are designed for about 25 °C, and their life falls sharply as temperature rises, commonly by about half for every 8 to 10 °C above that. A UPS room without reliable cooling can turn a five-year battery into a two-year one. Read more in our article on why UPS batteries die early in Saudi heat.

Lithium-ion UPS batteries tolerate heat better, last longer and take less space, at a higher upfront cost. Whichever you choose, include battery monitoring and a replacement plan in the design, because the battery is the part of a UPS most likely to fail when it is needed.
