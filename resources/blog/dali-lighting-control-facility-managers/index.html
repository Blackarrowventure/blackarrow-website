DALI Lighting Control: What It Actually Changes for Facility Managers

Addressable control versus switched circuits, and where the extra cost actually pays for itself

Black Arrow Venture company · Updated September 11, 2026

DALI gets pitched in almost every commercial lighting proposal now, usually as a line item rather than an explanation. Facility managers are often asked to approve the cost without a clear picture of what changes day to day once it's installed, versus a conventional switched or 0-10V dimming circuit. The difference is real, but it isn't the one most sales conversations lead with.

What DALI Actually Is

DALI (Digital Addressable Lighting Interface) is a two-wire digital protocol that gives every fitting on a circuit its own address, so a controller can talk to one luminaire, a group, or the whole circuit independently — set a level, query a status, or detect a lamp failure — over the same two wires that carry power to the ballast or driver. A conventional dimming circuit, by contrast, sends one analogue signal to everything on it: every fitting on that line does the same thing at the same time.

That single distinction — individually addressable versus one signal for everyone — is the entire technical difference, and it's what every practical benefit downstream actually comes from.

Where the Cost Actually Pays Back

The benefit facility managers notice first isn't dimming aesthetics, it's fault reporting. A DALI system knows when an individual driver has failed, because the fitting stops responding to its address — it shows up on a maintenance dashboard as a specific fixture at a specific location, rather than as a dark spot someone happens to notice on a walk-through. For a large floorplate or a multi-building site, that changes lamp-out response from reactive to proactive.

The second is genuine zoning without rewiring. Reconfiguring an office layout on a switched circuit means an electrician re-pulling cable to match new switch groups. On DALI, the same physical wiring can be regrouped in software, because the addressing — not the circuit topology — defines the zones. For a building that expects tenant turnover or repeated fit-outs, this is usually where the system pays for itself over a five-to-ten-year horizon.

Where It Doesn't Pay Back

A small single-tenant space with a stable layout and no facilities team monitoring a dashboard gets little of the above. The addressing and diagnostics are real capabilities, but they only generate value if someone is positioned to act on them — a warehouse with three light switches and one occupant doesn't need per-fitting fault reporting.

It's also worth being direct about integration cost: DALI does the lighting control cleanly, but connecting it into a broader BMS or IoT layer for occupancy-based dimming or daylight harvesting is a separate integration effort, not something DALI provides by itself. Specifications that promise smart-building outcomes from DALI alone are describing the wiring, not the outcome.

What to Check Before Specifying It

Confirm every fitting on the circuit actually supports DALI-2 (the current, interoperable version) rather than an older or proprietary variant — mixing generations on one circuit is a common cause of commissioning problems that get blamed on the wrong component. Also confirm who owns the commissioning: DALI's flexibility is only realised if someone actually programs the zones and scenes, and a system commissioned to factory defaults delivers none of the benefit described above.

Finally, ask what the maintenance contract actually covers. A DALI installation with no one monitoring the fault dashboard is paying for a capability nobody is using.
