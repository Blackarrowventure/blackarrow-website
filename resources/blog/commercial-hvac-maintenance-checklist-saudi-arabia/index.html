Commercial HVAC Maintenance Checklist for Saudi Buildings

Monthly, quarterly and yearly tasks for split, VRF, packaged and chilled-water systems, and why heat and dust change the schedule

Black Arrow Venture company · Updated November 10, 2026

In most of the world, air conditioning works hard for a few months a year. In Saudi Arabia it runs for most of the year, at outdoor temperatures near 50 °C, in air that carries fine dust. That changes what maintenance has to do. A schedule written for a mild climate leaves coils clogged and systems short on capacity just when the summer peak arrives. This checklist sets out what to check, and how often, for commercial buildings.

HVAC maintenance checklist

TaskFrequency in Saudi conditions
Inspect, clean or replace air filtersMonthly, and after dust storms
Check condensate drains, traps and pumpsMonthly
Check controls, set points and alarm logsMonthly
Clean condenser coilsQuarterly, more often in dusty sites
Tighten electrical terminals; check contactors and capacitorsQuarterly
Check refrigerant pressures, superheat and subcoolingQuarterly, by a qualified technician
Deep-clean coils, test safety controls, leak-test refrigerantYearly, before summer
Inspect ducts and insulation; calibrate sensorsYearly
Cooling tower water treatment and cleaningContinuous treatment, regular cleaning

Adjust frequencies to the manufacturer instructions and your site conditions.

Why Heat and Dust Change the Schedule

An air-cooled condenser rejects heat to the outside air. The hotter that air, the harder the compressor works and the less cooling it delivers; a layer of dust on the condenser coil makes it worse by blocking airflow. Units on roofs and in enclosed plant areas, where hot air recirculates, are hit hardest.

Dust also loads filters and evaporator coils quickly, especially after sandstorms, and long running hours wear contactors, capacitors, fan motors and belts faster. The result is that tasks done yearly elsewhere often need doing quarterly here, and filter checks monthly or more often in dusty seasons.

Monthly Checks

Inspect and clean or replace air filters; after dust storms, check them again within days. Check that condensate drains, traps and pumps flow freely, since a blocked drain is the most common cause of water damage from indoor units. Confirm thermostats and controllers show sensible set points and schedules.

Walk the plant: listen for unusual noise or vibration, check belts for wear and tension, look for oil stains that can signal a refrigerant leak, and record any alarm or error codes, especially on VRF systems, where the controller log is the earliest warning of a problem.

Quarterly Checks

Clean condenser coils, more often in dusty locations or after storms, using low-pressure water or a coil cleaner rather than high-pressure jets that bend the fins. Inspect evaporator coils and blower wheels. Tighten electrical terminals and check contactors and capacitors, since heat-related electrical faults are a leading cause of summer breakdowns.

Have a qualified technician check refrigerant operating pressures, superheat and subcooling, and the supply and return air temperature difference. These numbers show whether a unit is still delivering its rated cooling or slowly losing charge or airflow.

Yearly Checks

Before summer, do a full service: deep-clean coils, check fan motors and bearings, test safety controls and high-pressure cut-outs, leak-test the refrigerant circuit, and inspect ductwork and insulation for damage and condensation. Calibrate sensors and review building management system schedules so plant is not running for empty floors.

Chilled-water plants add their own tasks: chiller performance checks, pump and valve servicing, and above all cooling tower water treatment and cleaning to control scale and Legionella. Keep a written record of every visit; the standard ASHRAE 180 for inspection and maintenance of commercial HVAC systems is a useful framework for what to record.

What a Maintenance Contract Should Cover

A planned maintenance contract should list every asset by location and model, the tasks and frequency for each, response times for breakdowns by priority, what is included versus charged separately (refrigerant, parts, coil chemical cleaning), and the reports you receive after each visit.

Ask for measured readings, not just ticks: filter condition, refrigerant pressures, temperature differences and electrical readings over time. Trends in those numbers let you fix a failing unit in spring rather than replace it in August. For new installations, see our article on VRF and VRV systems for Saudi commercial buildings.
