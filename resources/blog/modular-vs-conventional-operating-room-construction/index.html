Modular vs Conventional Operating Room Construction: What Actually Changes

The real timeline difference, cleanroom classification, and where modular doesn't automatically win

Black Arrow Venture company · Updated September 11, 2026

Every modular operating room proposal leads with speed, and the speed claim is genuine — but it isn't the only thing that changes when a hospital chooses prefabricated wall and ceiling panels over conventional in-situ construction, and it isn't always the deciding factor it's presented as.

What 'Modular' Actually Means in an OR Context

Modular operating room construction uses prefabricated, cleanroom-grade wall and ceiling panels — manufactured and quality-tested off-site, then assembled on-site — in place of block-and-render walls finished with medical-grade coatings built up on site. The panels arrive with services (medical gas, electrical containment, isolated power conduit routes) pre-integrated into the panel design, rather than chased into finished walls after the fact.

This is a construction method, not a lesser standard: a properly specified modular OR meets the same NFPA 99, isolated power, and cleanroom requirements as a conventional build. What changes is where and how those requirements get built, not whether they apply.

The Timeline Difference, Realistically

The genuine time saving comes from parallel work: panel manufacturing and quality testing happen off-site while site works — structural, MEP rough-in, corridor finishes — proceed in parallel, rather than the sequential wait conventional finishing requires. For a hospital converting an existing space or adding a new theatre suite to an operating facility, compressing the construction window directly reduces lost operating days, which is usually the largest real cost of the project.

The realistic time saving is significant but rarely matches the most optimistic vendor claims, because commissioning, testing, and Ministry of Health / Civil Defense sign-off timelines don't compress just because the walls went up faster — those approval stages take the time they take regardless of construction method.

Cleanroom Classification and Why It's Not Optional

An operating theatre is typically specified to an ISO 14644 cleanroom class appropriate to the procedure type, which governs air change rates, filtration (HEPA at minimum for most OR classes), pressure differential to adjacent spaces, and surface finish requirements that prevent particle and microbial accumulation. Modular panel systems are generally easier to commission to a specific class because the seams, joints, and surface finishes are factory-controlled rather than dependent on site finishing quality — but the classification itself is a design requirement independent of construction method, and it has to be verified by actual particle-count testing after installation, not assumed from the panel manufacturer's datasheet.

This verification step gets skipped more often than it should, particularly on fast-track projects where the pressure is to open the theatre on schedule. A theatre that hasn't had its classification verified by commissioned testing is operating on an assumption, not a certified standard.

Where Modular Doesn't Automatically Win

For a single theatre in an existing building with irregular geometry, or where the room needs a genuinely custom layout that doesn't suit standard panel dimensions, the design flexibility of conventional construction can outweigh the timeline benefit of modular. Modular systems are most advantageous at scale — a multi-theatre suite where the same panel design repeats — and less advantageous for one-off, architecturally constrained spaces.

The decision is a genuine tradeoff between construction speed and design flexibility, not a case where one method is unconditionally better. A hospital facilities team is well served by having both options costed and timelined before committing, rather than treating modular as the default answer for every project.
