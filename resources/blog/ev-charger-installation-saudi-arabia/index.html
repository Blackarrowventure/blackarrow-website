EV Charger Installation in Saudi Arabia: Home & Commercial

Charger types and power levels, the supply check, protection and load management, from a single villa wallbox to a commercial charging site

Black Arrow Venture company · Updated November 10, 2026

Installing an EV charger looks simple: a box on a wall and a cable. In practice the charger is the easy part. What decides cost, timeline and safety is the electrical supply behind it, the route and size of the cable, the protection devices, and, on commercial sites, how many cars will charge at the same time. This guide walks through each step for homes and for commercial and public sites in Saudi Arabia.

AC or DC: Choosing the Charger

AC chargers (wallboxes) send alternating current to the car, which converts it on board. A single-phase 32 A charger delivers about 7.4 kW; three-phase chargers deliver 11 kW at 16 A or 22 kW at 32 A, although many cars accept only 11 kW on AC. AC charging suits homes, offices, hotels and anywhere cars park for several hours. The standard connector in Saudi Arabia is Type 2 for AC.

DC fast chargers convert power inside the charger and feed the battery directly, typically from about 30 kW to well over 150 kW, through the CCS2 connector. They suit highways, fuel stations, fleet depots and retail sites where cars stop for minutes, not hours, and they need a much larger supply, often with its own transformer.

The Supply Check Comes First

Before choosing a charger, confirm what the building can supply. New installations in Saudi Arabia use 230/400 V at 60 Hz, but some older buildings still run on the legacy 127/220 V system, which changes the charger choice and often means an upgrade. A load assessment then checks whether the main board and the distribution board feeding the charger have spare capacity for a load that may run at full current for hours.

If the existing supply is not enough, the options are a supply upgrade through the Saudi Electricity Company, a smaller charger, or load management that limits charging when the building is busy. Our article on MDB, SMDB and DB panels explains how this capacity is laid out in a building.

What the Installation Involves

Each charging point should have its own dedicated circuit from the distribution board, with a cable sized for continuous full-load current and for the length of the run, as international wiring rules for EV charging (IEC 60364-7-722) require. Protection normally includes a circuit breaker and a residual current device: a Type B RCD, or a Type A RCD with DC fault detection built into the charger.

Outdoor chargers need a suitable ingress rating, protection from direct sun where possible, and a mounting position that keeps the cable off the ground. Good earthing, correct labeling and a commissioning test that records insulation, earth loop and RCD trip results complete the job and form part of the handover file.

Commercial and Multi-Charger Sites

Once there is more than one charger, load management becomes the key design decision. Dynamic load balancing shares the available capacity between cars and against the building load, so ten chargers can run on a supply that could not feed all of them at full power. Chargers that support OCPP can connect to a back-office platform for user access, payment and energy reports.

Parking layout matters too: cable reach, bollards against vehicle impact, accessible bays, cable trenching across car parks, lighting and signage. For DC charging hubs the transformer, LV switchboard and civil works often cost more than the chargers themselves, so they should be planned first.

Compliance and Product Conformity

EV chargers sold and installed in Saudi Arabia must meet the SASO technical regulation for EV charging equipment, with conformity shown through the SABER platform. Ask for the certificate of conformity for the exact model before buying. Our article on SASO requirements for EV chargers in Saudi Arabia covers the details.

The main cost drivers are the charger power and quantity, the cable distance from the board, whether a supply upgrade is needed, civil works such as trenching and foundations, and any network platform. A short site survey answers most of these questions and turns a rough budget into a firm one.
