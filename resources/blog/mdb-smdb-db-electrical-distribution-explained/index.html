MDB, SMDB and DB Panels: Understanding Electrical Distribution Hierarchy

The path from utility supply to a wall socket, and why getting the hierarchy right prevents problems that show up years later

Black Arrow Venture company · Updated September 11, 2026

Facility drawings are full of three-letter abbreviations that get used loosely in conversation and precisely in the panel schedule: MDB, SMDB, DB. Getting the hierarchy straight matters beyond terminology, because each level exists to solve a specific fault-coordination problem, and skipping a level or sizing one wrong is a mistake that usually doesn't show up until the building is occupied and something trips that shouldn't.

The Hierarchy, From Utility to Socket

Power arrives at a building through the Main Distribution Board (MDB) — the first point of distribution downstream of the utility transformer or main incoming supply, carrying the building's full load and housing the main protective device. From the MDB, feeders run to Sub-Main Distribution Boards (SMDB), typically one per floor, wing, or major functional zone, each sized for the load of the area it serves rather than the whole building.

From each SMDB, final circuits run to Distribution Boards (DB) — the panels that actually feed individual circuits: lighting, sockets, small power, and specific equipment. A DB is the level facility staff interact with directly; an MDB is normally in a dedicated electrical room and rarely touched outside planned maintenance.

Why the Distinction Matters for Fault Coordination

The entire point of the three-tier structure is discrimination: when a fault occurs on a final circuit, only the DB breaker closest to the fault should trip, not the SMDB feeding it and certainly not the MDB feeding the whole building. Achieving that requires the protective devices at each level to be coordinated by time and current rating, not just individually rated for their own load.

A common and costly mistake is specifying breakers at each level that are individually correct for their own circuit but not coordinated with the level above — the result is a fault on one floor's lighting circuit tripping the SMDB for the whole floor, or in a badly coordinated system, the building's MDB. This is invisible on a single-line diagram unless someone actually runs the discrimination study, and it only becomes visible the first time a real fault occurs.

Sizing Mistakes That Show Up Years Later

SMDBs and DBs sized exactly for day-one load leave no room for the load growth that's normal over a building's life — added equipment, extra tenant fit-out circuits, EV charging retrofitted years later. When that headroom doesn't exist, expansion means either a new SMDB (disruptive and expensive to install into an occupied building) or overloading an existing board, which is where nuisance tripping and, in worse cases, thermal damage at connections starts to appear.

The standard practice of sizing distribution boards with 20-25% spare capacity and spare breaker ways exists exactly for this reason, and it's one of the cheapest insurance decisions in an electrical design, because the cost difference at design stage is small and the cost of a retrofit is not.

What a Load Study Actually Protects You From

A load study calculates actual expected demand — not just the sum of nameplate ratings of everything that could theoretically be connected — using diversity factors appropriate to the building type, and sizes each level of the distribution hierarchy against that realistic figure plus planned headroom. Skipping it in favor of a rule-of-thumb sizing is how boards end up either oversized (wasted capital cost) or undersized (the growth problem above).

For any facility expecting phased occupancy, tenant changes, or added equipment loads over its life — which in practice is nearly every commercial or healthcare facility — a proper load study at the SMDB and MDB level is not an optional design step, it's the calculation that determines whether the distribution hierarchy still works in five years.
