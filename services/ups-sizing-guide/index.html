How We Actually Size a UPS

Two numbers drive UPS sizing: your real critical load (measured, not estimated from equipment nameplates alone, which usually overstate actual draw) and how long that load needs to run on battery before either mains returns or a generator takes over. Undersizing either one is the common failure mode — a UPS that trips under load it should have handled, or a battery bank that runs out three minutes before the generator was supposed to start.

We start every sizing exercise with a load assessment rather than a capacity recommendation, then add headroom for future growth and account for battery derating in hot environments, since VRLA and lithium-ion batteries both lose usable capacity as ambient temperature rises above their rated range.

What Goes Into a Sizing Assessment

The Sizing Process

  • Real load measurement, not nameplate wattage alone
  • Required runtime, set against your generator start time or acceptable outage window
  • Headroom for future load growth, so the UPS isn't undersized within a year
  • Battery derating for ambient temperature, particularly relevant in Gulf climate battery rooms
  • Topology recommendation (online, line-interactive or standby) based on how sensitive the load actually is
Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Why would nameplate wattage give me the wrong UPS size?

Equipment nameplates list maximum rated draw, which is often well above what the equipment actually pulls in normal operation. Sizing off nameplate figures alone tends to produce an oversized, more expensive UPS — or, if someone under-corrects for it, an undersized one. A real load measurement gives a much more accurate number.

How much extra runtime should I plan for beyond my minimum requirement?

It depends on how confident you are in your generator's start time and how disruptive a shortfall would be. We typically build in headroom rather than sizing to the exact minimum, since batteries also lose some capacity over their service life — sizing to the bare minimum on day one means undersized runtime well before the battery's rated end of life.
